Sound as touch

UI feedback built from the speaker. Two layers: UI sound (what you hear) and haptic pulses (2-4 cycle sine bursts at the speaker's resonance, shaped so they move the chassis more than they make noise). Phones use the vibration motor when one exists. Everything is synthesized live.

Tuning

Saved per device. Rest your palms on the laptop while you tune: the right setting is where a pulse feels like a tap under your hand and barely sounds like anything. With headphones, you want a soft pressure push on the ears, not a boom.

Why it feels like vibration

  • Resonance, not bass. A laptop speaker and the plastic around it have a resonant band, usually 150-300 Hz. A burst right there moves the chassis far more than any other frequency, so you feel it in the palm rest. "Auto-find (mic)" measures that band with the built-in mic (hands off, room quiet); "Find by feel" sweeps it and you stop where your palms buzz most.
  • Few cycles, smooth window. A pulse is only 2-4 cycles with a raised-cosine envelope. Too short for the ear to hear a pitch, long enough for the body to feel a push. Hard edges are what make it sound like a click, so the haptic bus is also low-passed just above the pulse frequency.
  • Headphones. 35-55 Hz bursts at low level are felt as a pressure push on the ear and skull, the same trick as a phone's taptic tap. Less gain, slightly more cycles.
  • Sound helps the illusion. A tiny high tick in sync with the pulse makes the brain read both as one event coming from your finger (cross-modal binding). Turn UI sound off to compare.
  • Punch starts a pulse above the body frequency and drops into it. More punch = more like a kick drum (audible), less = more like a motor (felt).
  • Phones: navigator.vibrate where supported (Android Chrome). iOS Safari has no vibration API, so it uses sound only.
